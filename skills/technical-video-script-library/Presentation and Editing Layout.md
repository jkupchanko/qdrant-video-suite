# Presentation and Editing Layout

The story structure decides the order of ideas.
The presentation format decides how those ideas are delivered.
Choose both from the subject, audience and material available.

## Written hook

This is the short wording shown on screen at the opening.
It names the situation, question or useful result.
Keep it readable and consistent with the promise of the video.
It can be a short label while the spoken line supplies context.

## Verbal hook

This is the first spoken thought.
It gives the written hook meaning and creates the question the video will answer.
The two hooks should work together without repeating unnecessary wording.

## On camera

Use the speaker when their explanation, experience or response is the important part.
Bring in supporting material when a specific object or result is mentioned.
Keep the person and the relevant material easy to distinguish.

Plan where the face appears, how large it is and how often it returns to full screen.
Choose a default position and size for any small presenter window.
Keep that placement consistent through a continuous explanation.
Change the framing at a meaningful section boundary or when a supporting insert requires it.
Hide the face when it would cover code, results or another detail the viewer needs to inspect.

## Selective B roll

B roll is supporting material that makes a specific point easier to understand.
The main demonstration or walkthrough can carry the explanation without an additional insert.
Add a B roll block only where supporting footage or an existing asset adds useful context.
Do not add a block to every section or leave an empty block where no insert is needed.

For a five minute video, two brief inserts can be a useful starting choice.
Choose the actual number from the explanation and available material rather than a fixed quota.
Use an insert to clarify a mechanism, show a referenced object or resolve a point of confusion.
Keep a future animation idea separate from a request to capture an existing asset.

Each selected block should identify where to find the material, what to capture and the spoken line it supports.
Use an exact page, section, supplied asset or prepared application when available.
Keep these directions outside the spoken narration.

## Voiceover and walkthrough

Use narration over the actual task, screen or sequence of actions.
Follow one input or object through the process.
Leave enough time for the viewer to inspect the step being discussed.

## Conversation

Use a questioner and a guide, or two clearly named approaches.
Each question should represent a real point of confusion or decision.
Each answer should move the explanation forward.
Use role labels based on their job in the scene rather than judging their intelligence.

## Visual timeline

Use a sequence of dated or ordered events when their order explains the outcome.
Every event should change the situation or support the next decision.
Give text enough time to be read rather than imposing a fixed cut speed.

Add the following block only where a supporting insert is needed.

[**B roll:** **Find:** The verified page, section or supplied asset. **Capture:** The specific view or action needed. **Use during:** The spoken sentence that the insert explains.]

**Editor note, when needed:** [Give a specific instruction that helps the viewer understand the passage]

Use an editor note only when it adds something the Show and Transition directions do not already explain.
Put each visual direction directly beneath the passage it supports.
Keep background detail quiet enough for the important object to remain readable.
Attach an existing asset link to the relevant direction when one is supplied.

Camera describes the presenter’s framing.
Show describes the finished scene.
B roll describes the supporting material needed for a selected scene.
Those capture instructions can be passed to a separate recording workflow.

## Continuity

Keep the same object, example or question while developing an explanation.
When comparing, preserve the input or criterion so the difference is visible.
When moving forward, explain what the previous step produced and why the next step follows.
Return to the original question at the payoff.

## Writing voice

Use familiar words and specific verbs.
Write to one viewer doing one task.
Read the draft aloud and replace wording you would not naturally say.
Let a concrete observation create interest rather than promising a dramatic transformation.
Keep conditions beside the claims they qualify.
The finished script has one sentence per line and no source notes within the spoken passages.
