# The Problem and Breakthrough

**Use when:** A persistent problem became understandable after one useful discovery or change of approach.

**Audience fit:** Useful for diagnosis, customer problems, demonstrations and improvements to a process. The subject can be technical, practical or personal.

**Funnel fit:** Consideration or action. Use discovery when the problem itself is not yet familiar.

**Use another structure when:** The cause is already known and the viewer mainly needs the correction; use The Common Mistake.

**Keep it accurate:** Do not describe an untested idea as a breakthrough or hide a required step.

## Questions this structure answers

1. Why did the original approach fall short?
2. What changed the understanding?
3. How can the viewer check the new approach?

## General layout

The times show placement in a five minute video.
The fields describe what to write and show, rather than supplying a finished script.

### 1. Problem and hook   0:00 to 0:15

**Purpose:** Make the repeated problem visible and specific.

**Written hook:** Name the unresolved problem or the difference between the expected and observed result.

**Verbal hook:** State the attempted action and the result that still needs explaining.

**Template:** [Action] should produce [intended result], but [observed problem] continues.

**Keep the viewer with you:** Establish a discrepancy the rest of the video will explain.

[**Show:** The expected result beside the observed result. **Transition:** Keep the discrepancy visible while narrowing its cause.]

### 2. Cause and follow up   0:15 to 0:45

**Purpose:** Identify the assumption or stage that needs to be checked.

**Template:** [Observation] points to [specific assumption or stage].

**Keep the viewer with you:** Separate what is known from what is still a possible explanation.

[**Show:** The relevant detail, step or observation. **Transition:** Move from the observation to the deciding check.]

### 3. Discovery   0:45 to 2:45

**Purpose:** Explain the finding that changes the approach.

**Template:** [Check] reveals [finding], so [original assumption] needs to change.

**Keep the viewer with you:** Answer the opening question before introducing the fix.

[**Show:** The check and the finding it produced. **Transition:** Carry the finding directly into the changed action.]

### 4. Fix and check   2:45 to 4:25

**Purpose:** Show the action that follows from the finding and repeat the relevant test.

**Template:** [Changed action] addresses [cause]; repeating [test] shows [supported outcome].

**Keep the viewer with you:** A repair earns its conclusion through the check, not through confident wording.

[**Show:** The changed step and the repeated test. **Transition:** Return to the original discrepancy.]

### 5. Takeaway   4:25 to 5:00

**Purpose:** Give a useful rule and the conditions that matter.

**Template:** Check [specific cause] when [symptom] appears, then confirm [required result].

**Keep the viewer with you:** Close the problem with a check the viewer can reuse.

[**Show:** The original problem with its current result and one useful check. **Transition:** Finish on the practical check.]

## Adjust the depth to the length

**One minute:** Use one symptom, one cause, one fix and a repeat of the same check.

**Five to seven minutes:** Keep the full sequence and add only the explanation, demonstration and check needed for the stated goal.

**Thirty minutes:** Investigate competing causes, show failed checks honestly and verify that the repair holds across relevant cases.

## One example

This is an illustrative outline, not a recorded result or finished narration.

**Situation:** A file upload fails because the form and the server accept different file sizes.

Problem: Show the failed upload and the expected behavior.  
Cause: Inspect the size limits at both stages.  
Discovery: Identify the disagreement.  
Fix and check: Align the limits and repeat the same upload.  
Takeaway: Explain the need to check both sides of a shared requirement.
