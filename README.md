# Video Storytelling Structures

Ten general structures for explaining, teaching, comparing and telling a story.
Choose by the viewer’s question and the material available, then adapt the depth to the duration.

Each structure page explains what each section accomplishes, where the hook and follow up belong, what connects the parts and how visuals support the explanation.
The templates describe the layout rather than supplying a finished script.
One illustrative example sits at the bottom of each page.

## Choose the viewer before the structure

**Role:** A developer, marketer, customer, student or another clearly identified viewer.

**Experience:** What that person already knows about this subject.

**Funnel stage:** Discovery, consideration or action.

**Purpose:** What the viewer should understand, decide or be able to do.

**Available material:** What can be shown, explained, compared or checked.

**Length and format:** The requested duration and the way the explanation will be delivered.

[Compare audiences, experience and funnel stages](Audience%20and%20Purpose.md)

An experienced viewer may be discovering a new subject.
A newcomer may be ready to act.
Choose the reason to care from the viewer’s actual task.

## Ten structures

1. [The Journey](Styles/01%20The%20Journey.md)  
   Use when: A person or team faced a real obstacle, tried to overcome it and changed their approach.  
   Answers: Who wants what? What made it difficult? What changed, and what can the viewer learn?

2. [The Origin Story](Styles/02%20The%20Origin%20Story.md)  
   Use when: The viewer needs to understand why a person, team or service exists and what it is trying to do.  
   Answers: Where did this begin? What changed the direction? Why does this work matter to the intended audience?

3. [The Lesson](Styles/03%20The%20Lesson.md)  
   Use when: An experience, setback or unexpected result supports one useful lesson.  
   Answers: What happened? What did the person change? What should the viewer take from the experience?

4. [The Goal and Progress](Styles/04%20The%20Goal%20and%20Progress.md)  
   Use when: The work is still in progress and there is a meaningful goal, current obstacle or decision to show.  
   Answers: What are we trying to achieve? Where are we now? What needs to happen next?

5. [The Problem and Breakthrough](Styles/05%20The%20Problem%20and%20Breakthrough.md)  
   Use when: A persistent problem became understandable after one useful discovery or change of approach.  
   Answers: Why did the original approach fall short? What changed the understanding? How can the viewer check the new approach?

6. [The Practical Tip](Styles/06%20The%20Practical%20Tip.md)  
   Use when: One small action can be explained and demonstrated without a long background lesson.  
   Answers: What is the useful action? How do I perform it? How do I know it was done correctly?

7. [The Common Mistake](Styles/07%20The%20Common%20Mistake.md)  
   Use when: A repeated mistake has an observable consequence and a useful correction.  
   Answers: What is the mistake? Why does it cause a problem? What should I do differently?

8. [The Belief and Correction](Styles/08%20The%20Belief%20and%20Correction.md)  
   Use when: A familiar belief or tempting conclusion needs a counterexample and a more accurate rule.  
   Answers: What do people assume? What does the example actually show? What is a more useful rule?

9. [The Guided Process](Styles/09%20The%20Guided%20Process.md)  
   Use when: The viewer wants to complete a task with several necessary steps.  
   Answers: What will I be able to do? What must happen in what order? How can I check the result at each stage?

10. [The Comparison and Choice](Styles/10%20The%20Comparison%20and%20Choice.md)  
   Use when: The viewer must choose between two or three approaches, products or ways of working.  
   Answers: What are the choices? What makes one suitable for this situation? What should the viewer choose or test next?

## How a recommendation works

Start with any useful input: a verbal explanation, question, set of notes, article, draft, demonstration or existing video.
Identify the viewer, the purpose, the requested length and the material that can support the explanation.
Recommend one structure with a clear reason and offer an alternative only when its difference helps the choice.
Ask only the missing questions that would change the recommendation.

[Read the recommendation process](Recommendation%20Guide.md)

## Questions to clarify the brief

1. Who is watching, and what do they already know?
2. Are they discovering the subject, considering an approach or ready to act?
3. What should they understand, decide or do after watching?
4. How long should the video be, and is it a vertical short or a regular video?
5. What can we actually show or demonstrate?
6. Are the examples alternatives, dependent steps or illustrations of one idea?

Use answers already supplied instead of asking them again.

## Structure and presentation are separate choices

The structure decides the order of ideas.
The presentation may be on camera, narration over a walkthrough, a conversation or a visual timeline.
Distinguish the written hook on screen from the verbal hook.
Place filming and editing directions beneath the passage they support.
Plan full screen face appearances and keep any smaller presenter window consistent in size and position.
Add B roll instructions only at selected moments where supporting material clarifies the explanation.
Each selected insert identifies where to find the material, what to capture and the spoken line it supports.

## When there are several examples

Use The Comparison and Choice for alternatives that must be judged or selected.
Use The Guided Process for dependent stages of one task.
Use The Practical Tip for one small action illustrated with a few cases.
Keep a shared input or criterion visible when comparing, and a shared object visible when following a process.

## Match the depth to the length

**One minute:** Resolve one small question through one example and one useful condition.

**Five to seven minutes:** Include the explanation, demonstration or comparison needed to answer the central question.

**Thirty minutes:** Use chapters with meaningful intermediate results, checks and tradeoffs.

The times on the structure pages show a five minute placement example.
They are editing targets, not measured runtimes or a rule about cut speed.

## Where the structures come from

The library combines recurring habits from the reviewed popular videos with the organization shown in the supplied references.
It keeps concrete examples, connected progression, useful explanations and clear limits.
Popularity alone does not establish that a structure caused viewers to stay.
